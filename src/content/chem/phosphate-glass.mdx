After watching [this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jEOhfFwfdYA&t=199s)
from Apoptosis about his attempts at making Alums from rare earth elements, I was 
inspired to do some rare earth chemistry myself. I was particularly interested in
how the rare earth salts would change color depending on the kind of light they
were in, and wanted to see if I could make glass with this property. 
I read that phosphate glasses have a high solubility for rare earth ions
and aren't too difficult to make, so I decided to try it.

### What is Phosphate Glass?
Most glass is made from silicon oxide, where a bunch of silicon atoms are connected 
together by oxygen atoms.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/phosphate-glass/SiO2.svg" alt="Two-dimensional diagram of amorphous silica network" />
  <figcaption>Amorphous silica network. Image: Wikimedia Commons, public domain.</figcaption>
</figure>

It turns out you can replace silicon with
many other elements and still get something similar to normal glass. As an example,
strongly heating borax will cause it to decompose into boron oxide, which can 
form borate glass when it cools.

Phosphate glass is made by replacing 
silicon with phosphorus, meaning I'd need to melt down some phosphorus oxide.

##### Glass colors
If all normal glass is made of silicon oxide, how do we have so many different colors of glass? 
There are multiple ways to make colored glass, but a common one is to add a small amount of a 
transition metal oxide to the silicon oxide, where the metal you choose determines the color. 
For example, iron oxide gives a green color, and copper oxide make blue glass. 
For this project, I'd still be using metal oxides as dopants, but instead of transition metals, 
I'd use rare earth metals.

##### Other additives
Normal silicate glass often includes more than just the silicon and the coloring metal.
It's common to add some sodium oxide to lower the melting point, 
and calcium oxide to keep the glass from being water soluble. 
Aluminum oxide can be added to make the glass stronger. 
Many of these additives have a similar effect in phosphate glass, so I will using them as well.
The exact composition I went with was a modified version of the one described in 
[this paper](https://addi.ehu.es/bitstream/handle/10810/55938/1-s2.0-S0925838821044509-main.pdf;jsessionid=9BE82C910733A325BE94BEC53068BD47?sequence=1),
the main difference being that I replaced barium oxide with the less toxic calcium oxide.
It consists of[^true-composition]:
- 57% Phosphorus pentoxide
- 15% Rare earth oxide
- 10% Potassium oxide
- 8% Sodium oxide
- 5% Calcium oxide
- 5% Aluminum oxide

### Preparing the Rare Earth Salts
I decided to make glass doped with praseodymium, holmium, neodymium, europium, terbium,
gadolinium, and dysprosium. The first step was to convert the rare earth metals I had into 
salts that would dissolve in the glass melt. Oxides are what I really needed for the glass,
but carbonates would also work since they decompose into oxides at high temperature.
I figured the easiest way to do things would be to dissolve the metals in acid,
and then precipitate out insoluble carbonates by adding sodium carbonate.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/phosphate-glass/metals.jpeg" alt="Rare earths metals" />
  <figcaption>Getting ready to dissolve the metals</figcaption>
</figure>

Following Apoptosis, I
dissolved all but the europium in sulfuric acid, making the corresponding sulfate salts.
In hindsight, I probably should have used hydrochloric acid instead. 
Lots of rare earth sulfates have retrograde solubility, 
meaning they become less soluble as the temperature increases. 
Unfortunately, the process of the metal dissolving releases heat, 
so it took a very long time for the metals to dissolve. Since I had no plans
of making alums like Apoptosis, there was no benefit to making sulfate salts over chlorides.

Unfortunately none of the other rare earths had anywhere near as strong of a change.

After playing around with this for a bit, it was time to convert all my sulfate
salts into carbonates. I boiled them down for a while so I'd have less water to filter off,
and then added sodium carbonate to each solution. I only got video of this for holmium,
but the process was identical for all the other rare earths.

are colorless in normal light, but under UV light the terbium glass glows green and the europium glass glows red.
Surprisingly, the undoped phosphate glass glows blue under UV.

### What if we put it in a particle accelerator?
The legends at [Electron Impressions](https://www.youtube.com/@ElectronImpressions)
were kind enough to send some of the glass I made through an electron
particle accelerator, and the results were pretty cool. 

The glasses glows all different colors as they passed through the beam.

video

After, the glass was discolored, but returned to normal after a few days.

video

The terbium-doped glass was temporarily thermoluminescent, and would
glow green when heated. 

video

Surprisingly, the copper-doped borate glass seems to have become fluorescent, 
and glows yellow under UV light now. Previously it didn't glow at all under UV.
I'm not sure why this happened, my best guess is something related to 
some of the copper (II) getting converted to copper (I).

### Gold glass
As mentioned earlier, metal oxides are not the only way to make colored glass.
Another way is to use a metal directly, in the form of nanoparticles suspended 
in the glass. For a long time, red glass was made using gold nanoparticles, and
